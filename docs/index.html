01 / Dataset

A large population, a manageable sample.

The project studies yellow taxis. It does not describe all journeys in New York City.

Twelve monthly Parquet files contain 38,310,226 trip records. The analysis draws a fixed-seed sample within each pickup date and hour, then cleans the resulting 287,296 records. A total of 283,908 records remains for analysis.

Dataset componentCoverage and interpretation
Trip recordsFields describe pickup and dropoff times, zone IDs, distance, passenger count, payment type and charges. They describe recorded journeys rather than people seeking a taxi.
GeographyThe shapefile contains 263 taxi-zone records. IDs 264 and 265 represent unknown and outside-NYC locations and do not have polygons.
SamplingThe 0.75% sample preserves date-and-hour strata. Each month's sample share matches its share of the source population to within 0.01 percentage points.
Full-year estimatesSample counts and payment sums are divided by 0.0075. Averages, shares and ratios are not multiplied by this factor.
Time coverageThe report uses the recorded pickup and dropoff hours in the 2023 source files. It does not estimate waiting time before pickup.

The sample supports broad temporal and geographic comparisons. Rare routes and sparsely observed zones are less stable. The report does not attach confidence intervals to its scaled annual estimates.

02 / Method and engineering

Fix the records before interpreting them.

Cleaning decisions affect payments, tips and passenger counts as well as the size of the sample.

  1. Read and sample the monthly files.PyArrow reads Parquet metadata and pandas samples each pickup date-and-hour group with a fixed seed. This avoids loading the entire year's population into the exploratory workflow at once.
  2. Reconcile the schema and remove reversals.The January airport-fee field and the differently capitalised later field are combined. The offline-upload flag is removed. There are 2,855 records with negative charges; 87% match a corresponding positive record by time, zones and amount, supporting the reversal interpretation.
  3. Handle missing values without inventing payment behaviour.Missing passenger counts and rate codes use their modal values. Zero passengers are set to one, and rate code 99 is treated as missing. Payment type zero remains a separate category called "Not recorded".
  4. Infer missing fees from the charge breakdown.The residual between the total and known components is used to infer congestion and airport charges. This assumes the other charge components are complete. It is an imputation, not a directly observed fee.
  5. Remove implausible trips.Seven records with more than six passengers and 526 records with impossible combinations of distance, duration, fare or speed are removed. The rules include durations above five hours, distances above 250 miles and speeds above 80 mph.
  6. Calculate measures with the right denominator.Speed is total distance divided by total trip hours. Fare per mile is total fares divided by total miles. Recorded tip rate is total tips divided by total fares for qualifying credit card trips. These avoid unstable averages of per-trip ratios.
Technical layerLibraries and responsibilities
Data processingPython 3.12, pandas, NumPy and PyArrow support sampling, cleaning, grouping and Parquet storage.
Spatial analysisGeoPandas joins trip aggregates to taxi zones by LocationID and reads the public shapefile. The interactive map uses simplified copies of those boundaries.
ExplorationMatplotlib and seaborn produce the notebook's 27 saved figures. Jupyter records the code, outputs and interpretations together.
Report deliveryLocal Plotly.js, plain JavaScript, HTML and CSS produce the interactive report on GitHub Pages. Only aggregated data and public zone geometry are shipped to the browser.

This is an exploratory analysis, not a forecasting or dispatch-optimisation model. There is no trained predictive framework or measured intervention effect to report.

03 / Demand

The weekday commute and the weekend night are different markets.

Averages are normalised by the number of days in each group.

Weekdays build towards 18:00.

Pickups increase steeply from 06:00 to 08:00 and peak with the evening commute. Across all days, the 18:00 hour accounts for an estimated 2.68 million trips over the year.

Weekend activity extends later.

Average midnight pickups are about 2.5 times the weekday level. Weekend mornings rise about two hours later. These patterns support different schedules by day type rather than one fixed daily supply profile.

May and October are the busiest months, with about 3.47 million estimated trips each, while August has about 2.79 million. Estimated recorded payments total approximately $1.10 billion. These amounts include recorded fees and tips and are not driver profit or net operating revenue.

04 / Geography

Manhattan and the airports dominate the observed market.

Use the map to distinguish pickup sources from dropoff destinations.

JFK Airport is the busiest individual pickup zone, with 5.2% of sampled trips. Eight of the ten busiest zones are in Manhattan. Sparse yellow taxi pickups elsewhere do not prove that travel demand is low; travellers may use other modes or find taxis unavailable.

JFK remains busy from the afternoon to midnight, with pickups peaking at 22:00. Many Manhattan zones peak earlier. A citywide curve is therefore not enough to decide where a taxi should wait.

Airport flows are asymmetric.

JFK has about 4.29 pickups per dropoff and LaGuardia has 2.51. East Elmhurst, near LaGuardia, has 8.24. These ratios identify repositioning questions, but the records do not measure how taxis reached those zones.

Residential destinations have the opposite pattern.

Roosevelt Island records about 0.04 pickups per dropoff. Low ratios can make a return pickup less likely within the same yellow taxi market. Actual empty mileage would need vehicle-linked trajectories to verify.

05 / Speed and price

The busiest part of the day is slow and expensive per mile.

These are observed associations. Trip length, geography and fare rules also change through the day.

Average speed falls from 25.0 mph at 05:00 to 10.9 mph at 17:00.Fare per mile rises from $4.37 at 05:00 to about $6.00 in the afternoon. Metered time charges are consistent with this pattern, but the analysis does not isolate congestion's causal contribution.

The congestion surcharge appears on 92.3% of cleaned trips. The extra charge is concentrated from late afternoon through the night, consistent with rush-hour and overnight rules. Charge frequency is not a direct measure of congestion intensity.

06 / Passenger behaviour

Payment coverage changes what can be inferred about tips.

Recorded tips are observed only for credit card transactions.

For qualifying card trips, recorded tips equal 22.3% of fares. The ratio is 26.3% for trips of up to two miles and 20.0% for trips over five miles. This is a share of total fares, not the average passenger's chosen percentage, and a lower percentage does not necessarily mean a smaller dollar tip.

Single-passenger trips represent 77.4% of the cleaned sample, and the mean group size is 1.38. Since the fare is largely independent of passenger count, sharing the same fare reduces the cost per passenger. That arithmetic does not, by itself, establish demand for a new group product.

07 / Conclusion

Use the patterns to design tests, not to claim an optimum.

The report identifies where an operational decision deserves closer examination.

What the year of records establishes.

The cleaned sample shows a clear weekday evening peak, stronger weekend late-night activity and a concentrated yellow taxi geography. Airports are major pickup sources, while some residential zones are primarily destinations. Lower daytime speeds coincide with higher fares per mile, and recorded tip rates vary with trip length.

These findings support time-specific and location-specific planning. They do not establish that more taxis, different fares or a particular repositioning policy would improve outcomes. Those decisions require demand, queueing, driver cost and service-quality information that the trip records do not contain.

Limits that matter to a decision-maker.

  • Completed trip records do not measure unmet demand, failed hails, passenger waiting or actual empty driving.
  • A 0.75% sample is useful for large patterns but unstable for rare routes. The five-trip route threshold is a screening rule, not a guarantee of precision.
  • Passenger and fee imputations influence the corresponding charts. Cash tips remain unobserved, so card-tip findings should not be generalised to every passenger.
  • The observations are from 2023. Current fare rules, travel behaviour and competing services may differ.

Technical handover and reproducibility.

The repository separates data download, the analysis notebook, written methods and the browser report. Re-running the notebook regenerates the cleaned sample and static figures. The report exporter then creates aggregate JavaScript data from the cleaned sample and public zone boundaries; it does not publish trip-level records.

  • NYC Taxi and Limousine Commission trip records provide the source data and dictionaries.
  • The saved notebook and methods notes record the transformations and definitions.
  • The report exporter and dependency manifest support reproduction in the existing Python environment.
